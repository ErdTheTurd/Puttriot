Not on the App Store yet

Puttriot is in App Review. There is no public listing to install today. The button says “Coming soon to the App Store” and stays on this website until the listing is live.

This page is the manual, written so the behavior is described before you are standing on a green with a tee time. If a shipped version disagrees with a sentence here, the app in your hand is the thing to trust and this page gets corrected. We would rather fix a sentence than leave you with a confident wrong one.

Which device you need

Puttriot runs on iPhone and iPad. A live read — scanning a real green and drawing the line — is a different thing from the demo green and the speed tools. Only LiDAR devices do the live read.

You do not need the newest Pro to get LiDAR. A 12 Pro still has the scanner. You do need a device that can run the iOS version the App Store will require. When the listing exists, that listing is the authority on the minimum system, not this paragraph.

Storage is ordinary for a small utility: the app itself, plus the practice putts you choose to keep. A scan of one green is not a video you are meant to store. If a device is already full of photos, clear some room before you blame the reader.

The demo green

The demo green is a practice surface inside the app. You can use it indoors. You can use it on an iPhone or iPad that does not have LiDAR. It is how you learn the screen before you are on a real green.

It is not a read of your carpet, your office, or a green you played last week. If you wanted a line on real grass and the device has no LiDAR, the honest answer is that this app will not draw one.

Duo cover display

On iPhone, the Duo cover display shows the read summary. It is a short view of the read you already have. It does not walk the green for you.

Tour

Tour is optional. It is a subscription: monthly, yearly, or lifetime. Apple sells it and bills it. Puttriot does not make an account for it. Tour unlocks extras in the app.

This page does not list prices or invent the extras. When the App Store listing is public, that listing is where both are spelled out. If a sentence here ever disagrees with the listing, the listing wins and this page gets edited.

Before you scan

Pick a putt you can walk. Eight to thirty feet is the range where a read earns its keep. Inside four feet you can usually see the break with your eyes, and the phone is mostly in the way. Past forty feet you are often dealing with more than one slope, a tier, or a putt you should be lagging anyway. The app can still look, but do not expect one clean aim point to describe a putt that changes its mind halfway.

Know which end is the ball and which end is the hole before you start. It sounds obvious. People still start at the hole and then read the break backwards. Walk from the ball toward the hole, the way the ball will roll.

Wipe the lens. A thumbprint turns a flat green into a funhouse. Take the case off if it shadows the camera or the LiDAR window. The LiDAR window is the black dot near the cameras on the back of a Pro iPhone, not the flash. On an iPad Pro it sits with the rear cameras.

You do not need a tripod, a stake, or a marked ball. You need enough light to see the green yourself. If you cannot see the hole clearly, do not expect a clean end to the walk.

Walking the putt

Set the ball first. Then walk toward the hole at a walking pace, phone pointed down at the grass, not at the horizon. The scanner needs to see the ground. A pretty photo of the flag against the sky is the wrong picture.

Cover the ground the ball will cross, plus a little to either side. Break lives in the slope beside the straight line, not only under the string. If you only scan a six-inch ribbon, you will miss the tilt that actually moves the ball.

Do not rush the ends. The ball and the hole are the two points the line has to join. When you reach the cup, tap Find hole. Then stop. Extra loops around the green do not make a truer putt, and they do make a muddled surface.

If someone walks through the putt while you are scanning, start over. A caddie, a mower, or your own shadow parked on the line will get built into the ground.

Ball and hole

You show the app both ends. Set the ball where the putt starts. Tap Find hole when you are at the cup. There is no requirement to use a particular brand of ball, a striped ball, or a ball with a sensor in it. A coin or a tee can stand in for the ball on the practice green if you are not putting a real one.

The hole is the hole. If you are practicing to a tee or a chalk mark instead of a cup, mark that point as the hole and remember you are practicing to a spot, not to a cup that can swallow a ball. Pace suggestions that say “die it in the hole” assume a cup. A tee in the ground will not take the ball.

Do not move the ball after you have marked it and then keep the old read. The line is between those two points. Move either one and the putt is a different putt.

LiDAR, or no live read

A live read needs the LiDAR scanner and ARKit, on the devices listed above. LiDAR measures distance and gives ARKit a surface to reconstruct. A green is a shallow shape. A one-percent slope over ten feet is a little over an inch of fall. Eyes miss that. The scanner is what Puttriot uses to see it.

There is no camera-only live read. A non-Pro iPhone does not get a quieter, worse line on real grass. It gets the demo green and the speed tools. We do not want a camera guess wearing a LiDAR coat.

On a LiDAR device, if the read looks wrong, scan again before you trust it. A second pass that matches the first is worth more than the first pass alone. If the scanner and your feet disagree, believe your feet until a new scan agrees with itself.

The line

The line is a path from the ball to the hole over the surface the device built. On a flat green it is nearly straight. On a slope it bows. The bow is the break.

Read the bow from the ball’s point of view, the way you will stand. “Breaks right” means the ball will move to your right as it rolls away from you, and you start it to the left to allow for that. “Breaks left” is the mirror. If you are standing at the hole looking back at the ball, left and right swap. That is the most common way to talk yourself out of a correct read. Turn around. Look from the ball.

A line that kinks hard in the middle usually means the green does, or the scan jumped. A tier, a repaired pitch mark, or a seam between two passes of the mower can be real. A kink that appears and disappears when you scan again was the scan.

The aim point

The aim point is not the hole. It is where you want the ball to start. On a right-breaking putt it sits left of the hole. You pick a blade, a discolored patch, or an old ball mark near that point and roll the ball over it. The break is then supposed to bring the ball back to the cup.

Fig. A on the home page is the picture: ball, aim point off the curve, hole at the end of the line. The aim point is at the start of the putt, not a mark beside the cup on the high side. People mix those up when they are nervous.

Uphill putts break less than the same slope downhill, because the ball slows sooner. Downhill putts take more line and less pace. If the app’s aim point and your eyes are in a fight on a downhill slider, give the extra line a serious look before you talk yourself into the hole.

Contours

The bands on the green are slope lines, kin to the rings on the icon. Closer bands mean the slope is changing faster. A wide quiet middle means the putt is flatter there. They are an overlay so you can see the shape that produced the line. They are not a substitute for the line. If the bands and the line tell two stories, scan again. One of them is confused, and it might be both.

Do not try to putt the bands. Putt the aim point.

Pace and stimp

Stimp is how far a ball rolls on a flat green when it is released from the standard ramp. A slow public green might be an 8. A decent club green is often around 9 to 11. A fast tournament green can be 12 or 13, sometimes more. Those are golf’s numbers. They are not a promise about what this app measured.

Puttriot has green-speed presets. Pick the one that matches the green you are standing on, or enter a number you actually know. The phone does not measure stimp off a picture of the grass. A preset is a starting point. If the superintendent has a number posted, use that.

Apple WeatherKit can adjust that speed for the weather. To do it, the app uses an approximate location, rounded to about 1 kilometer, and asks Apple for a forecast. That request is for the weather, not a map of your rounds. You can ignore the adjustment and keep the number you chose. If you refuse location, the presets still work.

Weather data provided by Apple Weather.

If you do not know the speed, do not pick the fastest preset because it sounds professional. Use a slower preset for an ordinary park or municipal green unless the surface is obviously quick, and a quicker one for a private club green that feels slick under your feet. Then call the pace a model.

Pace in the app is a suggestion tied to that number and to the slope. “Die it in the hole” means the ball arrives with just enough speed to fall, if the line is right. That is a sound pace on a flat putt and on most downhill putts, where anything firm will run. Uphill, a ball that is dying at the hole often dies short of it, so a firmer roll is the usual fix. Read the words on the screen. Firm means the ball should still be moving when it gets there. Die means it should be nearly done.

A one-stimp error is enough to change a ten-footer. If your putts keep coming up short and the line is good, the speed you entered is probably too fast, or you are quitting on the stroke. The phone cannot see a quit. It can only do the arithmetic you handed it.

Grain, the way the grass grows, is not the same thing as slope. Into the grain is slower. Down-grain is quicker. Bermudagrass in the afternoon can have more grain than slope. The scan sees shape. It does not see which way the blades lean. On grainy grass, treat pace as a hint and watch a couple of putts before you trust it.

What “model” means

Some numbers are measurements. A stimp you rolled with a stimpmeter is a measurement. A length you taped is a measurement. The slope from a finished LiDAR scan is a measurement of the surface the device built, which is close to the green and not the same thing as a survey.

A model is what the app computes from those inputs: the curve, the aim point, the suggested pace. It is a physics sketch of a golf ball on a tilted plane, with the speed you entered standing in for how fast the grass is. A weather adjustment is still a model. It is a forecast, not a stimpmeter. Balls are not perfect, greens are not glass, and you do not strike the ball in the center every time. The model does not know your miss.

When the app marks a figure as a model, that is the honest label. Do not scrape the word off in your head and call it a measurement. Fig. A on the home page is a diagram of the shape of an answer. It is not a read of a green you are standing on, and it is not a measured putt.

Light, weather, and the grass

Bright overcast is a gift. You can see the surface, and the scanner can see the grass. A low sun behind the hole will flare the lens. Turn so the sun is off the glass. LiDAR cares less about the sun than your eyes do, and it still needs a clear view of the ground. A thumb, a case, or your hand over the LiDAR window will punch a hole in the surface.

Dew makes a green slower than yesterday’s stimp. Rain on the surface does the same, until the green firms up. If yesterday’s number is still in the app and the green is wet, the pace will be too firm and the ball will die early. Change the speed or ignore the pace and keep the line. The weather adjustment is a nudge from the forecast, not a promise it felt the dew.

Wind does not move a rolling ball as much as people think, until it does, on a slick downhill putt. The app ignores wind. You should not, on those putts.

A recently topdressed or punched green will scan lumpy because it is lumpy. The line may chatter. That is the green. Practice your lag and come back when it has healed. Do not demand a tour read off a green that is full of sand.

When the read looks like junk

Scan again before you edit your swing around a wild line. Most bad reads are one of these:

  • You walked too fast, or you pointed the phone at the flag instead of the grass.
  • The lens is dirty, or a finger covered the LiDAR window.
  • Someone crossed the putt mid-scan.
  • You marked the hole and the ball the wrong way round.
  • You are reading left and right from the hole, not from the ball.
  • The speed is a fantasy. A very fast preset on a shaggy public green will tell you to barely touch a putt that needs a firm stroke.
  • The green is in pieces: a tier, a bunker lip, a drain. One aim point cannot describe it. Break the putt into two looks, or putt it as a lag.
  • The phone is hot, in low-power mode, or the camera is in use by something else. Close the other app. Let the phone cool off in the shade. A hot phone throttles the sensors.

Two scans that agree are a read. Two scans that disagree are a question. Take a third. If the third matches one of them, use that. If all three disagree, put the phone away and putt with your eyes. The green did not fail. The scan did.

Practice history

You can keep a putt and look at it again. That is for the twelve-footer you want to see twice, not for a diary of every three-footer. The history lives with the app, on the device. This website does not have your putts. See the privacy policy for what stays on the device.

A saved putt is the read you stored, not a promise the green still falls that way tomorrow. Dew, a mower, and a new hole location all change it. If the cup moved, the old read is a different putt. Scan the new one.

Juniors

A junior can use a parent’s phone or iPad on the practice green. There is no account, no nickname, and no photo of the kid. Hand over the device, let them walk the putt, then take it back. The value for a junior is seeing that break is a shape, not a guess you apologize for. The risk is believing the screen so hard they stop looking. Make them call the break out loud before they turn the screen around.

Competition

This is a practice tool. The Rules of Golf restrict what you may use during a round. A device that measures slope and tells you a line is exactly the sort of thing a committee may prohibit, and many already do, under the rules on equipment and on advice. Local rules sometimes allow distance-measuring devices and still forbid measuring elevation. Read the notice, or ask the person who posted the draw.

We will not publish a “tournament mode” that pretends a measuring device is suddenly legal because we hid a button. If you are not sure, leave the device in the bag.

What it will not do

  • It will not do a live read on a device without LiDAR. There is no camera scan of a real green.
  • It will not read a putt from a photograph you took last week.
  • It will not measure stimp off the grass. Presets and a weather adjustment are not a stimpmeter.
  • It will not see grain.
  • It will not see wind.
  • It will not putt the ball, pick a club, or caddie a full hole.
  • It will not turn a bad stroke into a good one. A perfect line with a shoved putt still misses.
  • It will not replace looking at the putt from both sides, which still works and does not need a battery.

The device itself

A long practice session in the sun will warm the phone or the iPad. Heat makes devices dim the screen and slow the sensors. Shade it between putts. Do not leave it face up on the dash of the cart.

Low Power Mode can limit the camera and ARKit. Turn it off while you practice, then turn it back on if you want it. A live read asks for the camera, because LiDAR rides with that camera system, and for motion so the walk can be followed. The weather adjustment asks for location. The app rounds that location to about 1 kilometer and uses it for Apple WeatherKit. It is not a record of which course you played. If you refuse location, set the speed yourself.

If the device asks for your contacts, your photo library, the microphone, or Bluetooth, deny it and check this page. Those are not used. Update iOS when you can. ARKit changes with the system. An ancient iOS and a new app are a bad pair, and the App Store will usually refuse the install before you have to debug it.
